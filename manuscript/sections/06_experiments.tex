The experiments answer three questions.
How tight are the bounds, and how short can the start-up window be?
How does C-RDD compare with other methods?
Does each part of C-RDD help?

\subsection{Instances and Settings}
\label{sec:exp:setup}

\textbf{Instances.}
Each instance has $m \in \{2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 15, 20\}$ sites served from one depot.
Six robot types serve duties of $6$ or $10$ hours and have maintenance cycles of $2$ to $7$ duties.
Each site allows two or three random types and lies $0$, $1$, $2$ or $4$ hours from the depot.
In family K1 every site needs one robot; in family K12 a site needs one or two.
All durations are rounded up to slots of $2$ hours, so a schedule that is feasible on slots is feasible in real time.
For each $m$ and family we generated $16$ instances: $6$ for training and $10$ for testing.
All parameters were set on the training instances; this section reports only the $160$ test instances.

\textbf{Methods.}
C-RDD uses the settings of Section~\ref{sec:method}: early overhauls in $[0, 2S_{\max})$, a deviation window of $0.5 S_{\max}$, $\rho = 0.1$, $\gamma = 0.4$, a $(4 + 12)$ evolution strategy, $T_{\mathrm{fit}} = 6 S_{\max}$ and $G_{\mathrm{stall}} = 30$.
Here $S_{\max}$ is the longest maintenance cycle among the robot types, about two weeks in our instances.
We compare C-RDD with seven methods.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Dispatching rules: phase balance (PB), which sends the robot whose phase is least served; five rules evolved by genetic programming (GPHH)~\cite{zhang2024survey} on the training instances; and an evolution strategy (ES) that tunes the weights of a dispatching rule for each instance.
  \item Decoders: a random-key evolutionary algorithm (RK-EA)~\cite{londe2025brkga}, an evolutionary algorithm with one gene per dispatch decision (DD-EA), and RDD with a hand-written rule~\cite{rdd}.
  \item A rolling-horizon mixed-integer program (RH-MIP) that fixes the fleet in its first step and then plans eight overlapping horizons.
\end{itemize}
All methods share the simulator, the tool assignment rule and the fleet search: start from a fleet, add resources while sites run short, and remove resources while the schedule stays feasible.

\textbf{Evaluation.}
Every final schedule is checked for the infinite horizon: it must repeat a cycle with no uncovered slot, or run $600{,}000$ slots (about $137$ years) with none.
A run that ends without such a schedule counts as unsolved.
Each run has a wall-clock limit on one core: $300$\,s for $m \le 4$, $600$\,s for $m = 6$, $1800$\,s for $m = 8, 10$ and $3600$\,s for $m = 15, 20$.
The limit includes all preprocessing, such as the linear program and the entering construction.
% TODO：补机器型号与编译器。
Search methods run five seeds per instance; PB, RH-MIP and the entering construction run once, and each GPHH rule runs once.
Results are averaged over the solved runs of an instance.
The gap is the excess over the lower bound $\lceil \max_H R_0(H) \rceil$, in percent.
A method is compared with C-RDD on the instances that both solve, by the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Holm correction.
For the Friedman ranks, an unsolved instance takes the worst rank.

\subsection{Bounds and the Time Bound}
\label{sec:exp:bounds}

Table~\ref{tab:bounds} lists the bounds.
The start-up bound lies $2.4$--$8.3\%$ above the rate bound.
Without early overhaul the start-up cost is $4.1$--$11.4\%$; early overhaul in the start-up window removes $27$--$45\%$ of it.
The start-up cost is largest on small instances, where a few posts cannot be staggered well.

Fig.~\ref{fig:lpconv} shows how the start-up bound grows with the horizon $H$.
It reaches $97$--$99\%$ of its final value at $H = S_{\max}$ and more than $99\%$ at $1.5 S_{\max}$, for every $m$.
The start-up cost is therefore decided within about one maintenance cycle, as Theorem~\ref{thm:enter} suggests.
For $m = 20$ the bound still grows slightly between $3 S_{\max}$ and $4 S_{\max}$.

Table~\ref{tab:window} varies the deviation window on the instances with $m = 4$ and $6$.
A window of $0.25 S_{\max}$ or $0.5 S_{\max}$ gives the same results.
Longer windows give worse results: with the same $\rho$, more genes act on later decisions.
A deviation window of about one week is enough.

\subsection{Comparison}
\label{sec:exp:compare}

Table~\ref{tab:compare} compares C-RDD with each method.
On the instances with $m \le 6$, C-RDD is better than every method on most instances, and no method beats it on more than three of the $20$ instances of any size.
ES is the strongest competitor; its gap is $2.5$--$3.6$ points larger.
RDD with a hand-written rule is next, $3.3$--$7.0$ points behind, and its distance grows with $m$.
The rules (PB, GPHH) and RH-MIP are $9.5$--$19$ points behind.
DD-EA needs one gene per decision; its fleet search often runs out of time before the search starts, so it solves only $24\%$ of the runs at $m = 6$.

\subsection{Ablation}
\label{sec:exp:ablation}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} removes one part of C-RDD at a time.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Without the relaxation rule}, the deviations act on a hand-written rule (RDD). The gap grows by $3.3$--$7.0$ points.
  \item \emph{Without the entering construction}, the rotation rule runs from the fresh start and the search must fix the start-up alone. The gap grows by $2.3$--$3.2$ points.
  \item \emph{Without the search}, the best entering construction is returned. The gap grows by $1.3$--$2.7$ points. The construction alone is better than the search without the construction.
  \item \emph{With a short fitness horizon} of $720$ hours, the search sees too little of the start-up and stops at larger fleets. The gap grows by $1.8$--$1.9$ points.
\end{itemize}
